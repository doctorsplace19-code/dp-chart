DOT Physical Vision and Hearing Requirements Explained

Vision and hearing are two of the most commonly misunderstood parts of the DOT physical. Here is exactly what the FMCSA standards require, what happens if you fall short in one eye or ear, and how the exemption and alternative-standard pathways actually work.

Medically reviewed by Chantal Gabriel, MD Updated September 2026

Most drivers walk into a DOT physical worried about blood pressure or a drug test, and are caught off guard when the vision and hearing checks turn up an issue instead. Both standards are federal, both are specific down to the number, and both have a real pathway forward if you do not meet them outright — something a lot of drivers do not realize until they are already sitting in the exam chair.

Key point: The vision and hearing standards are set out in 49 CFR 391.41(b)(10) and (b)(11). Falling short in one eye or ear does not automatically end your certification — there are federally defined pathways for both. When you're ready, order your DOT physical online.

The FMCSA vision standard, in plain numbers

To be certified under 49 CFR 391.41(b)(10), a driver must have, with or without corrective lenses:

  • Distant visual acuity of at least 20/40 in each eye individually.
  • Distant visual acuity of at least 20/40 in both eyes together (binocular).
  • A field of vision of at least 70 degrees in the horizontal meridian in each eye.
  • The ability to recognize the standard red, green, and amber colors of traffic signals and devices.

If you need glasses or contacts to hit those numbers, that is normal and does not disqualify you — the medical examiner will simply note "qualified only if wearing corrective lenses" on your medical certificate, and you must wear them any time you operate a commercial motor vehicle.

What if you don't meet the standard in your worse eye?

This is where most confusion comes from. FMCSA replaced its old vision waiver programs in 2022 with an alternative vision standard, codified at 49 CFR 391.44, for drivers who cannot meet the distant acuity or field-of-vision standard in one eye — including drivers who are functionally monocular.

To qualify under the alternative standard:

  • Your better eye must independently meet the standard vision requirements above on its own.
  • A licensed ophthalmologist or optometrist must evaluate you and complete the Vision Evaluation Report, Form MCSA-5871, before your DOT physical.
  • Your DOT physical must begin within 45 days of the date the specialist signs that report.
  • A road test may be required unless you already have at least three years of intrastate or specific excepted interstate CMV driving experience with the vision deficiency, hold a valid federal vision exemption, or are already medically certified under a related FMCSA provision.

The medical examiner reviews the specialist's report alongside your exam and certifies you if you meet the criteria — there is no separate application to FMCSA headquarters for most drivers using this pathway.

The 2026 change: grandfathered vision waivers are gone

If you were certified decades ago under FMCSA's original vision waiver study program (1992–1994) or the later vision exemption program (1998–2022), you may have been operating under a "grandfathered" status that let you skip re-applying every time the rules changed. FMCSA formally eliminated that grandfathering provision, effective March 23, 2026, because the current alternative vision standard under 391.44 has fully replaced those older programs. If you were relying on a grandfathered status, talk to your medical examiner about transitioning to the current 391.44 pathway well before your next certificate expires.

The FMCSA hearing standard

Under 49 CFR 391.41(b)(11), a driver is physically qualified for hearing if they meet either of these, with or without a hearing aid:

  • Forced whisper test: You perceive a forced whispered voice in your better ear at not less than 5 feet.
  • Audiometric test: Your average hearing loss in the better ear is no more than 40 decibels at 500 Hz, 1,000 Hz, and 2,000 Hz.

The whisper test is the standard screening method at most DOT physicals. If you do not pass it, the examiner can move to an audiometric test, which measures your hearing threshold at those specific frequencies rather than relying on a subjective response.

Hearing aids and the FMCSA hearing exemption program

Hearing aids are explicitly allowed for meeting the standard — if you pass with one in, the medical examiner notes on your certificate that a hearing aid is required while driving. For drivers who are deaf or hard of hearing and cannot meet the standard even with a hearing aid, FMCSA runs a separate federal hearing exemption program. It remains active: FMCSA has continued to grant and renew individual exemptions in 2026 for drivers who demonstrate a safe driving record and CMV proficiency, typically issued in two-year terms. Unlike the vision alternative standard, the hearing exemption is a direct federal application processed through FMCSA, not something your local medical examiner can grant on the spot.

How to prepare if you have a known vision or hearing issue

  1. If you wear corrective lenses or a hearing aid, bring them to your DOT physical — testing is done with them in place.
  2. If you have vision only in one eye or significantly reduced vision in one eye, schedule your ophthalmologist or optometrist evaluation (Form MCSA-5871) before your DOT physical, not after, and book your exam inside the 45-day window.
  3. Bring documentation of your CMV driving history if you are relying on the three-year experience exception to skip a road test.
  4. If you hold an existing federal vision or hearing exemption, bring the exemption letter to every DOT physical until it expires.
  5. Do not assume an old grandfathered status still applies — confirm your current pathway with your medical examiner given the March 2026 rule change.

For a broader look at what else can trigger a deferral at a DOT physical, see our guide on what disqualifies you from a DOT physical.

Bottom line: Vision and hearing standards are specific and federal, but neither one is automatically disqualifying if you fall short in one eye or ear. The alternative vision standard and the hearing exemption program both give drivers a documented path to certification — the key is starting the paperwork with a specialist before your DOT physical, not scrambling after a deferral.

Frequently Asked Questions

What visual acuity do you need to pass a DOT physical?

At least 20/40 distant visual acuity in each eye, with or without corrective lenses, plus 20/40 binocular acuity with both eyes together and a field of vision of at least 70 degrees in the horizontal meridian in each eye. You must also be able to recognize red, green, and amber traffic signal colors.

Can you get a DOT medical card with vision in only one eye?

Yes, through the FMCSA alternative vision standard under 49 CFR 391.44. You need an evaluation from a licensed ophthalmologist or optometrist on Form MCSA-5871, your better eye must independently meet the vision standard, and depending on your driving history you may need to complete a road test.

What is the DOT hearing test standard?

You must perceive a forced whisper in your better ear at 5 feet, with or without a hearing aid, or pass an audiometric test showing no more than a 40-decibel average hearing loss in the better ear at 500, 1,000, and 2,000 Hz.

Are hearing aids allowed during a DOT physical hearing test?

Yes. FMCSA rules specifically allow the hearing standard to be met with or without a hearing aid, and the medical examiner will note on your certificate that a hearing aid is required if that is how you passed.

What happened to the old FMCSA vision waiver and exemption programs?

FMCSA phased out the legacy vision waiver study program and vision exemption program in favor of the alternative vision standard adopted in 2022, and it formally removed the outdated grandfathering provision for old program participants effective March 23, 2026.
